## Key Takeaways

- **Capital compounding is the main pattern:** Revenue grew from $215.4B in FY2016 to $371.4B in FY2025, while shareholders' equity grew from $282.1B to $717.4B. Equity compounded faster than revenue, showing that retained earnings and investment marks matter more than sales growth alone.

- **Operating earnings became sturdier, but GAAP net income stayed noisy:** Operating income grew at an 8.6% CAGR from FY2016-FY2025, while net income swung from a FY2022 loss to $96.2B in FY2023 and $67.0B in FY2025. Berkshire should be read through operating earnings, not headline GAAP profit.

- **Liquidity is now a strategic asset:** Cash and short-term investments rose from $86.4B in FY2016 to $373.3B in FY2025, a 17.7% CAGR. The opportunity cost is visible in slower FCF growth, but the option value is central to Berkshire's risk posture.

- **Free cash flow is useful but incomplete:** FCF grew only 2.8% annually from FY2016-FY2025 and fell sharply in FY2024 before rebounding in FY2025. For Berkshire, FCF mixes insurance, railroad, utility and investment activity, so it should cross-check rather than replace operating earnings.

- **Insurance remains the flywheel:** The FY2024 filing memo shows float of about $171B, GEICO's combined ratio improving to 81.5%, and insurance investment income rising 44.6%. This gives Berkshire low-cost capital and makes underwriting discipline thesis-critical.

- **The conglomerate is deliberately uneven:** Revenue is spread across insurance, manufacturing, distribution, retail, BHE and BNSF, but pre-tax earnings power in FY2024 was concentrated in insurance underwriting, insurance investment income, manufacturing, BNSF and BHE.

## The Business

Berkshire Hathaway is a decentralized holding company. It owns insurance and reinsurance businesses, BNSF Railway, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, and a broad portfolio of manufacturing, service, retailing, distribution and leasing subsidiaries. Headquarters is unusually small; subsidiary managers run operations, while the parent controls major capital allocation, investments and CEO selection.

The economic engine is insurance float plus retained earnings. Disciplined underwriting can create low-cost or negative-cost float, and Berkshire invests that capital across public equities, Treasury Bills and controlled businesses. Outside insurance, the railroad and utility businesses add durable but capital-intensive earnings, while the operating subsidiaries provide diversified cash generation.

The business is not a normal conglomerate where revenue mix explains value creation. Capital allocation, liquidity, insurance underwriting discipline and subsidiary autonomy are the core drivers.
