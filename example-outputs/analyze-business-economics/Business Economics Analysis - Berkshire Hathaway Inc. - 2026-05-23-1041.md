# Berkshire Hathaway Inc. - Business Economics Analysis

**Date:** 2026-05-23
**Ticker:** BRK-B
**Business Model:** Insurance-led decentralized conglomerate

---

## Executive Summary

### Quick Reference

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| What type of business? | Insurance-led decentralized conglomerate: insurance float and retained earnings fund a portfolio of wholly owned businesses, public equities and Treasury Bills. |
| What's the key metric? | Float cost and underwriting discipline, led by combined ratio across GEICO, Berkshire Hathaway Primary Group and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group. |
| What's misleading in GAAP? | GAAP net income is dominated by investment gains and losses; Berkshire's FY2024 net income was $88.995B while operating earnings were $47.437B. |
| What multiple to use? | Sum-of-the-parts anchored on adjusted book value and normalized operating earnings, with P/B as the insurance sanity check. |
| Who are true peers? | Markel, Fairfax Financial and Alleghany-in-structure historical references; operating peers vary by segment. |
| Biggest analytical mistake? | Treating Berkshire like a normal consolidated industrial company where revenue, EBITDA and FCF cleanly explain value creation. |

### The Big Quirks

1. **Investment mark-to-market through earnings** - Equity securities create large GAAP earnings swings even when operating businesses are stable. FY2024 net income was $88.995B, but operating earnings were $47.437B because investment gains and losses are economically different from recurring subsidiary earnings.
2. **Float is both liability and capital source** - Insurance liabilities look like obligations, but disciplined underwriting can make them low-cost or negative-cost funding. Berkshire's float was about $171B at FY2024 year-end.
3. **Reserve estimates and catastrophe timing** - Current earnings can include prior-year reserve development, catastrophe losses and loss-estimate changes that do not describe current accident-year underwriting quality.
4. **Book value is useful but imperfect** - Berkshire's equity securities, AOCI, operating subsidiaries, deferred taxes and repurchase behavior make book value directionally important but not a full intrinsic-value proxy.
5. **Consolidated FCF blends unlike economics** - Insurance, BNSF, BHE, manufacturing, distribution and retail have different cash conversion patterns, so reported FCF is a cross-check rather than the core earnings measure.

### What to Focus On

- Combined ratio and float cost - determines whether Berkshire is paid to hold investable insurance funds.
- Operating earnings by category - strips out market marks and shows the earnings power of insurance, BNSF, BHE and operating subsidiaries.
- Cash, Treasury Bills and equity securities - shows option value, risk capacity and opportunity cost.
- Shareholders' equity growth per share - long-term compounding record, especially when paired with repurchases.
- Segment capital intensity - BNSF and BHE require heavy reinvestment, unlike much of the insurance investment engine.

### What to Ignore

- GAAP net income in isolation - large investment gains and losses swamp year-to-year operating progress.
- Consolidated revenue mix - McLane and Pilot are large revenue contributors but much less important to earnings power than insurance, BNSF and BHE.
- EV/EBITDA - Berkshire's insurance liabilities, cash pile, equity portfolio and regulated subsidiary debt make enterprise-value framing weak.
- Single-year FCF - FY2024 FCF fell to $11.616B, but the decline says less about intrinsic earnings than it would for a simple industrial company.

## 1. Business Model Classification

**Primary Category:** Insurance-led decentralized conglomerate.

**Secondary Characteristics:** Capital allocator, P/C insurer and reinsurer, regulated railroad, regulated utility owner, industrial holding company, public equity portfolio and Treasury Bill liquidity platform.

**How This Business Makes Money:**
Berkshire earns money from insurance underwriting, insurance investment income, BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, manufacturing, service, retailing, distribution and equity-method affiliates. The distinguishing feature is that insurance float and retained earnings provide long-duration capital that can be invested in public equities, Treasury Bills and wholly owned businesses. In FY2024, the most important economic contributors were insurance underwriting EBT of $11.405B, insurance investment income EBT of $16.748B, BNSF EBT of $6.648B, BHE EBT of $2.292B and manufacturing EBT of $11.895B.

## 2. Economic Engine

| Driver | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Primary Cash Source | Operating earnings from insurance underwriting, investment income, BNSF, BHE and manufacturing, plus float-funded investment income and portfolio liquidity. |
| Core Unit of Value | Dollar of float held at low or negative cost, then redeployed by Berkshire into securities, cash equivalents or controlled businesses. |
| Growth Drivers | Insurance premium growth with underwriting discipline, investment yield on float and cash, retained earnings, opportunistic acquisitions, subsidiary reinvestment and occasional buybacks below intrinsic value. |
| Margin Drivers | Insurance pricing and loss cost discipline, GEICO expense efficiency, BNSF operating efficiency, regulated utility returns and mix away from low-margin distribution revenue. |
| Capital Intensity | Mixed but high in aggregate: insurance can be capital-efficient when float is profitable, while BNSF, BHE and many operating subsidiaries require large maintenance and growth capex. |
| Reinvestment Requirements | BNSF and BHE require recurring asset-base reinvestment; manufacturing/service subsidiaries need maintenance capital; insurance requires statutory capital and conservative liquidity; excess capital can remain in Treasury Bills until opportunities appear. |

**Economic Engine Summary:**
Berkshire is best understood as a compounding machine rather than a consolidated operating company. The insurance group can generate investable float at attractive cost, and the parent allocates that capital across public equities, Treasury Bills, acquisitions, repurchases and subsidiary reinvestment. The economic question is whether underwriting discipline and capital allocation remain strong enough to offset the drag from very large cash balances and capital-intensive regulated assets.

**ROIC Calculation Note:**
The FY2024 10-K memo calculated directional ROIC at 18.3% using pre-tax operating earnings before equity-method earnings, an estimated tax rate and average invested capital defined as shareholders' equity plus consolidated debt less cash, equivalents and Treasury Bills. That is useful as a rough cross-check, but Berkshire's float, insurance liabilities, large equity portfolio and non-recourse BHE/BNSF debt mean ROIC should not be used like a simple industrial ROIC.

**Seasonality & Working Capital Timing:**
Insurance premium cash receipts, claim payments, catastrophe losses, Treasury Bill repositioning, BNSF/BHE capex and portfolio sales can all distort quarterly cash flow. Period comparisons should separate operating earnings by segment from investing and financing choices, then review changes in float, cash/Treasury Bills and equity securities.

## 3. Key Accounting Quirks

### Quirk 1: Investment Mark-to-Market Through Earnings

**What:** Under GAAP, changes in fair value of equity securities flow through earnings. Berkshire also reports operating earnings to help users separate operating business results from investment gains and losses.

**Distortion:** Net income can swing dramatically with market prices. In FY2024, Berkshire reported $88.995B of GAAP net income and $47.437B of operating earnings, so headline earnings overstated recurring operating earnings by $41.558B.

**Adjustment:** Start analysis from operating earnings and then value the equity portfolio separately. Use GAAP net income mainly to understand total economic book-value movement, tax effects and market exposure.

**Example:** The FY2024 10-K synthesis says Buffett emphasized operating earnings because investment gains and losses create large period-to-period volatility.

### Quirk 2: Reserve Estimation and Prior-Year Development

**What:** Insurance claim liabilities are estimates. Prior-year development, catastrophe losses and reserve changes flow through current-period underwriting results.

**Distortion:** Calendar-year underwriting results can look better or worse than current accident-year economics. BH Primary's FY2024 result deteriorated partly because favorable prior-year development declined and GUARD reserve estimates increased.

**Adjustment:** Track current accident-year results separately when disclosed, review 5-year reserve development, and treat recurring favorable reserve releases as lower-quality earnings than current-year underwriting profit.

**Example:** Berkshire's FY2024 memo identifies property and casualty claim liabilities as a critical estimate and flags BH Primary reserve estimate pressure.

### Quirk 3: Float Economics

**What:** Float is policyholder money held before claims are paid. It appears as insurance liabilities, but if underwriting breaks even or better, the float can function like low-cost funding.

**Distortion:** GAAP separates underwriting results and investment income, which can obscure that insurance liabilities help fund investment assets. A combined ratio below 100% means underwriting profit before investment income, or negative-cost float.

**Adjustment:** Calculate float cost from underwriting profitability and track float size against shareholders' equity. Berkshire's FY2024 float was about $171B, and its average cost of float was negative in both 2024 and 2023.

**Example:** GEICO's FY2024 combined ratio was 81.5%, BH Primary's was 95.4%, and BHRG P/C's was 82.9%.

### Quirk 4: AOCI and Book Value Volatility

**What:** Available-for-sale securities and other comprehensive income items can move book value even when operating earnings are stable.

**Distortion:** Book value changes can reflect interest-rate and market movements rather than business performance. Conversely, wholly owned subsidiaries may be worth more or less than their carrying values.

**Adjustment:** Use shareholders' equity growth as a long-term compounding indicator, but adjust interpretation for AOCI, equity marks, deferred taxes and repurchases. Do not equate book value mechanically with intrinsic value.

**Example:** The FY2024 memo shows shareholders' equity of $649.368B and equity securities of $271.588B, with 71% of equity securities fair value concentrated in five companies.

### Quirk 5: Reinsurance and Gross-Net Exposure

**What:** Reinsurance can reduce net losses and premiums while creating recoverables and counterparty exposure. Reinsurance economics differ from primary insurance and can carry low-frequency, high-severity risk.

**Distortion:** Net underwriting figures can hide gross catastrophe exposure, counterparty recoverability and the timing of loss recognition. Berkshire also writes unusually large risks that many insurers avoid.

**Adjustment:** Monitor gross versus net exposure, recoverables, catastrophe loss load and management's stated appetite for single-event pre-tax losses. Berkshire tries to avoid single-event pre-tax losses above $15B, per the FY2024 memo.

**Example:** BHRG P/C remained profitable in FY2024 despite $800M of significant catastrophe losses.

### Quirk 6: Consolidated Cash Flow Mix

**What:** Berkshire's cash flow statement combines insurance, railroad, utility, manufacturing, distribution, retail, financing and investment portfolio activity.

**Distortion:** Operating cash flow and FCF do not map cleanly to owner earnings. FY2024 OCF was $30.592B and FCF was $11.616B, far below operating earnings of $47.437B, partly because the consolidated cash flow statement blends businesses with very different economics.

**Adjustment:** Use FCF as a liquidity and reinvestment cross-check, not as the primary valuation input. Analyze BNSF/BHE capex separately from insurance investment income and parent-level liquidity changes.

**Example:** FY2024 capex and equipment held for lease was $18.976B, while cash/Treasury Bills rose to $334.2B.

## 4. Metrics That Matter

| Metric | Why It Matters | Where to Find It | Benchmark |
|--------|----------------|------------------|-----------|
| Combined Ratio | Measures underwriting profitability before investment income; below 100% means float is not costing Berkshire money. | 10-K MD&A insurance underwriting detail; annual report insurance tables. | <95% excellent; 95-100% acceptable; >100% unprofitable underwriting. |
| Loss Ratio | Shows claims severity and pricing adequacy; GEICO loss ratio improvement was central to FY2024. | 10-K insurance underwriting discussion; GEICO segment detail. | 55-65% typical P/C; <55% unusually favorable and may normalize; >70% concerning for personal auto without offsetting price. |
| Expense Ratio | Captures insurance operating efficiency; GEICO's FY2024 expense ratio was 9.7%. | 10-K insurance underwriting tables. | <15% excellent for direct auto; 15-25% acceptable depending on line; >30% concerning unless specialty pricing offsets it. |
| Current Accident-Year Loss Ratio ex-Cat | Strips out prior-year development and catastrophes to reveal current underwriting quality. | Insurer supplements when disclosed; for Berkshire, request in annual report detail or compare commentary. | Stable or improving year over year is good; >3pt deterioration is watch; >5pt deterioration is concerning. |
| Current Accident-Year Combined Ratio ex-Cat | Best normalized underwriting profitability measure across periods. | Insurer supplements when disclosed; annual report commentary. | <92% excellent; 92-96% good; >96% concerning. |
| Catastrophe Load | Measures severity and risk concentration; Berkshire accepts more mega-cat risk than most insurers. | 10-K catastrophe commentary and reinsurance underwriting discussion. | 3-6 pts typical for broad P/C; >8 pts elevated; >10 pts recurring is concerning. |

| Float Leverage | Shows how much investable insurance funding supports equity. | Float disclosure and shareholders' equity. | 0.2-0.5x conservative for Berkshire-like conglomerate; >1.0x increases insurance balance-sheet sensitivity; >1.5x concerning for Berkshire's model. |

| Book Value Per Share Growth | Long-term compounding scorecard, especially when repurchases are rational. | Annual report, balance sheet and share count. | >10% CAGR strong; 5-10% adequate; <5% weak. |
| Tangible Book Value Per Share | Helps adjust for goodwill/intangibles from acquisitions. | 10-K balance sheet. | Growth near BVPS is fine; >5pt annual lag vs BVPS is watch; persistent decline is concerning. |
| Operating ROE | Cleaner measure than GAAP ROE because it excludes some market volatility. | Operating earnings and average shareholders' equity. | >15% excellent; 12-15% good; <10% inadequate. |

| Cash + Treasury Bills | Measures optionality, risk-bearing capacity and acquisition firepower. | Balance sheet and capital allocation section. | >$30B required by policy; >$100B fortress; <$50B would be a major Berkshire-specific watch item. |
| Operating Earnings by Category | Shows which engines are actually producing value. | 10-K operating earnings reconciliation. | Broad-based growth good; >50% from one source is concentration watch; decline in insurance plus BNSF/BHE would be concerning. |

**Primary KPIs to Track:**

1. Combined ratio and float cost - this determines whether Berkshire earns investment returns on free or negative-cost capital.
2. Operating earnings by category - this is the cleanest recurring earnings view for Berkshire's mixed business base.
3. Cash/Treasury Bills plus equity securities - this shows liquidity, optionality and exposure to market marks.

## 5. Metrics to Ignore or Adjust

| Metric | Problem | Alternative |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| GAAP net income | Includes investment gains/losses that can overwhelm operating performance. | Use operating earnings plus separate portfolio valuation. |
| Consolidated revenue growth | Large low-margin distributors can move revenue without moving intrinsic value much. | Track operating earnings by category and capital employed. |
| EV/EBITDA | Enterprise value is poorly defined for an insurer with huge cash, float, equity securities and non-recourse regulated subsidiary debt. | Use sum-of-the-parts, adjusted book value and operating earnings. |
| Reported FCF yield | Cash flow mixes insurance, capex-heavy rail/utility reinvestment and investment portfolio activity. | Use normalized owner earnings and segment-specific reinvestment analysis. |
| Segment operating margin on revenue | Insurance, BNSF, BHE, McLane, Pilot and manufacturing have unlike economics. | Compare each segment to its own relevant metric: combined ratio, OR/capex, allowed returns, distribution margins. |

**GAAP Net Income Issues:**
FY2024 GAAP net income of $88.995B was not the best read on Berkshire's recurring earnings because investment gains and losses flow through earnings. Operating earnings of $47.437B better captured insurance underwriting, insurance investment income, BNSF, BHE and operating subsidiary performance. Use GAAP net income for total book-value reconciliation and tax/accounting review, not as the headline earnings base.

**GAAP Cash Flow Issues:**
FY2024 operating cash flow of $30.592B and FCF of $11.616B were much lower than operating earnings. That gap does not automatically indicate poor earnings quality because Berkshire's cash flow statement combines insurance working capital, claims timing, regulated and railroad capex, portfolio repositioning and subsidiary reinvestment. The correct response is segment-level cash conversion, not a blanket FCF multiple.

**Standard Ratio Assessment:**

| Ratio | Usability | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| P/E | Adjust | Use only on normalized operating earnings; avoid raw GAAP P/E because investment marks dominate. |
| EV/EBITDA | Avoid | Insurance float, cash/T-Bills, equity securities and non-recourse subsidiary debt make EV/EBITDA misleading. |
| P/B | Useful | Relevant as insurance/financial-company anchor, but adjust for equity portfolio marks, deferred taxes, wholly owned subsidiaries and repurchases. |
| FCF Yield | Low | Useful as a stress and reinvestment cross-check, not primary valuation. Normalize segment cash conversion. |

## 6. Common Analytical Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It's Wrong | Correct Approach |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| Valuing Berkshire on raw GAAP P/E | Investment gains/losses can make earnings surge or collapse without comparable operating change. | Use operating earnings and value the investment portfolio separately. |
| Treating Berkshire as a simple conglomerate | Revenue mix understates insurance, BNSF and BHE economic importance and overstates low-margin distribution. | Use a capital allocation and segment economics lens. |
| Treating cash as lazy excess without considering option value | Large Treasury Bill balances reduce near-term returns but protect underwriting capacity and preserve acquisition optionality. | Estimate opportunity cost and option value separately. |
| Applying industrial FCF yield mechanically | BHE/BNSF capex, insurance timing and investment activity distort consolidated FCF. | Use normalized owner earnings and segment-specific reinvestment assumptions. |
| Assuming GEICO's FY2024 result is fully normalized | An 81.5% combined ratio is exceptional and may reflect pricing/frequency conditions that normalize. | Model GEICO through-cycle combined ratio and policy-count growth tradeoffs. |

**Red Flags That Aren't Red Flags:**
- Very large Treasury Bill balances - this can look like under-deployment, but Berkshire's model values safety, opportunistic capital and insurance risk capacity.
- GAAP net income below the prior year despite stronger operating earnings - this can happen when investment gains are lower.
- Low consolidated revenue growth - value can compound through operating margin, investment income, equity portfolio returns and buybacks even with flat revenue.

**Green Flags That Aren't Green Flags:**
- Huge GAAP net income from investment gains - this may simply reflect market appreciation and can reverse.
- Strong FCF in one year - portfolio activity and working-capital timing can make a single year unrepresentative.
- Revenue growth from low-margin distribution - McLane or Pilot sales growth is not equivalent to high-return capital compounding.

**Quantified Risk Impacts:**

| Risk | Calculation | Impact |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Insurance mega-loss | $15B single-event pre-tax loss threshold / $649.368B FY2024 shareholders' equity | About 2.3% of equity before tax, material but survivable at Berkshire scale. |
| GEICO normalization | 5pt combined ratio deterioration x $42.252B FY2024 GEICO premiums earned | About $2.1B less pre-tax underwriting earnings. |
| Cash opportunity cost | 100 bps lower yield x $334.2B FY2024 cash/Treasury Bills | About $3.3B less pre-tax investment income. |
| Equity portfolio drawdown | 20% drawdown x $271.588B FY2024 equity securities | About $54.3B pre-tax mark-to-market hit before tax effects. |

## 8. Valuation Implications

**Preferred Valuation Methods:**

1. Sum-of-the-parts anchored on adjusted book value - best reflects the equity portfolio, cash/T-Bills, insurance float, BNSF, BHE and operating subsidiaries.
2. Normalized operating earnings plus portfolio value - useful when separating recurring earnings from mark-to-market movements.
3. P/B with ROE adjustment - important insurance-led sanity check, but not enough by itself because Berkshire owns many non-insurance operating businesses.

**Multiples Assessment:**

| Multiple | Usability | Notes | Benchmark Range |
|----------|-----------|-------|-----------------|
| P/E | Medium | Use only on normalized operating earnings, not GAAP net income. | <16x cheap, 16-22x typical, >24x expensive for mature high-quality compounders. |
| EV/EBITDA | Avoid | EV and EBITDA are weak concepts for Berkshire's insurance/float/cash/equity structure. | N/A |
| EV/EBIT | Low | More useful for operating subsidiaries than consolidated Berkshire. | N/A |
| P/FCF | Low | Consolidated FCF is noisy and capex mix differs sharply by segment. | N/A |
| P/B | Medium | Useful insurance anchor; adjust for equity marks, deferred taxes and subsidiary intrinsic value. | <1.2x cheap, 1.2-1.6x typical, >1.8x expensive for Berkshire-like quality. |
| Price / Normalized Operating Earnings | High | Better than GAAP P/E because it excludes investment mark volatility. | <14x cheap, 14-20x typical, >22x expensive. |
| SOTP Premium / Discount to Adjusted Book | High | Best consolidated method when segment values can be estimated. | >10% discount cheap, +/-10% fair zone, >20% premium expensive. |

**DCF Considerations:**

| Issue | Implication | Adjustment |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| Catastrophe normalization | One year can overstate or understate underwriting earnings. | Use through-cycle cat load and reserve development assumptions. |
| Equity portfolio marks | Market values are observable but volatile. | Value portfolio separately at market value, then apply tax/liquidity judgment. |
| BNSF/BHE capital intensity | Growth requires large reinvestment and regulatory access. | Model maintenance and growth capex separately from insurance earnings. |
| Cash drag and option value | Treasury Bills reduce near-term compounding but increase downside resilience and acquisition optionality. | Model base yield on cash plus optional deployment scenarios. |
| Terminal value | A perpetuity on consolidated earnings hides portfolio and float economics. | Use terminal adjusted book/SOTP multiple rather than simple earnings perpetuity. |

## 9. Seasonality & Working Capital

Berkshire has multiple timing layers rather than one clean seasonal pattern. Insurance premiums, claims and catastrophes can create underwriting timing; BNSF and BHE require steady capital spending; manufacturing, retail and distribution carry ordinary working-capital cycles; and parent-level cash changes can reflect Treasury Bill and equity portfolio decisions. Quarterly analysis should therefore compare segment operating earnings, float, cash/T-Bills, capex and equity securities separately.

Key period-comparison rules:

- Compare insurance underwriting by combined ratio, loss ratio, expense ratio and catastrophe load before reading consolidated cash flow.
- Compare BNSF and BHE after separating maintenance needs, growth investment, interest expense and regulatory cost recovery.
- Compare parent liquidity by cash/Treasury Bills, equity securities, buybacks and acquisitions rather than by operating cash flow alone.
